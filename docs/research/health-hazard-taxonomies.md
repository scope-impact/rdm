# Health/safety hazard-category taxonomies: what the standards actually supply

Research note for **RDM-004.12**. One question:

> Does an existing standard supply a hazard-category taxonomy for health/safety
> harms in software, structured the way STRIDE is — a closed set generated by
> negating a property set — or must one be adapted?

**No taxonomy proposed here.** The ticket puts taxonomy *content* out of scope
(it is an `RMP-001` revision). This note establishes only what prior art exists,
what shape it has, and what adopting or adapting it would cost.

## Answer, up front

1. **No health/safety hazard taxonomy with STRIDE's generative structure was
   found in any standard.** ISO 14971:2019 and ISO/TS 82304-2 both require
   hazards to be identified and documented; neither supplies a closed set, and
   ISO 14971's four category *headings* are enumerative, physical-device-shaped,
   and explicitly example lists.
2. **The STRIDE pattern — a closed set generated by negating a property set —
   does exist in safety engineering, twice, and neither instance is a hazard
   *category* taxonomy.** Both are *identification* guide-word sets applied per
   data flow or per control action: **SHARD/HAZOP** (5 guide words) and **STPA's
   unsafe control actions** (4 types). The pattern also replicates once outside
   safety, in **LINDDUN** (7 privacy threats), which was built deliberately as
   STRIDE's privacy sibling.
3. **The Five Rights of CDS is a design heuristic, not a safety taxonomy.** Its
   negation is used *rhetorically* in the CDS literature and in vendor/trade
   writing ("the right information to the wrong person at the wrong time is
   noise"), but no peer-reviewed source was found that publishes the negated
   Five Rights as a hazard or failure-mode taxonomy. The published CDS-safety
   taxonomies classify **causes** (build error, conceptualization error, …), not
   harms.
4. **A standards committee has already tried and abandoned a generative error
   taxonomy in this space.** IEC 62366:2007 carried a slip/lapse/mistake use-error
   taxonomy; IEC 62366-1:2015 removed it, reportedly because the class
   distinctions were not reliably discriminable in practice. That is the single
   most load-bearing precedent in this note for what a generative taxonomy costs.
5. **Classification and identification artefacts in this field are cleanly
   split, and the split runs the way the ticket predicts.** Identification
   artefacts are long, open, example-shaped, and used once per design element
   (ISO 14971 Annex C, ISO/TR 24971 Annex A, HAZOP/SHARD, STPA). Classification
   artefacts are filing structures for incident reporting after the fact (WHO
   ICPS, Magrabi et al., NCC MERP, Health IT Hazard Manager) — none of them
   built for a designer to file a *pre-market design risk* against.

## 1. ISO 14971:2019 Annex C, and where the prompt list actually went

### 1.1 The hazard tables: what they are

`[SRCH]` Annex C of ISO 14971:2019 is titled **"Fundamental risk concepts"**. It
carries Figure C.1 — the hazard → foreseeable sequence of events → hazardous
situation → harm chain, with `P = P1 × P2` — and one or more tables of
**"Examples of hazards"**.

The categories those tables are organised under are, consistently across digests:

1. **Energy hazards** (electrical, mechanical, thermal, radiation)
2. **Biological and chemical hazards** (cross-contamination, biocompatibility)
3. **Operational hazards** (improper functionality, including from user error)
4. **Information hazards** (insufficient or confusing instructions for use)

So there *are* four category headings. Four facts about them matter:

- **Table numbering is unresolved.** One digest attributes energy hazards to
  **Table C.1**, another to **Table C.3**, and a third names **Table C.3** as
  *biological and chemical* hazards. A redistributed excerpt is titled
  `List-of-Hazards-Table-C.1-ISO-14971.pdf` but headed *"Excerpt – ISO 14971:2019,
  Table C.3 — Examples of hazards Energy hazards"* — the file itself disagrees
  with its own filename. **Do not cite a table number from this note.** What is
  well corroborated is the four category names, not their numbering.
- **The content is examples, not members.** Every digest describes Table C.1 as
  a list of *things* — "electricity, moving parts, infectious bacteria,
  chemicals, gases, sharp edges, high currents, temperature, ionizing radiation"
  — i.e. energy and material sources. The four headings are a way of grouping
  examples; the examples are the payload.
- **The construction principle is physical, not logical.** One digest describes
  the method as *"starting from a (simplified) physical quantity and then
  identifying circumstances through which it can develop its effect into
  damage"*. That is the opposite of STRIDE's construction: STRIDE starts from a
  *property to preserve* and negates it; ISO 14971's tables start from an
  *energy or substance present in the device* and trace it forward to harm.
  There is no property set being negated, so there is nothing that closes the
  set.
- **Annex C is informative.** Annex C is not where 14971's requirements live —
  the hazard-identification *requirement* is a numbered clause (5.4 in the 2019
  edition) `[SRCH]`. Nothing found suggests 14971 anywhere requires hazards to
  be *categorised*, only identified. `[LOCAL]` corroborates the shape from the
  2007 edition: rdm's own audit checklist for ISO 14971:2007 lists
  `14971:4.3 Identification of hazards` as the obligation, with no category
  requirement anywhere in its 33 items. (rdm's `14971_2019.txt` checklist is an
  unwritten stub — `# TODO: write this checklist` — so it contributes nothing.)

**Verdict:** open, enumerative, physically-derived, ~4 headings over dozens of
examples, and intended for **identification**. Not a filing taxonomy.

### 1.2 The prompt list moved out of the standard entirely

`[SRCH]` The long question list that used to be **ISO 14971:2007 Annex C**
("Identification of hazards and characteristics related to safety") is, in the
2019 revision, **ISO/TR 24971:2020 Annex A** — a separate, non-normative
Technical Report. Digests report:

- ISO/TR 24971:2020 has **eight annexes, A–H**; Annex A ≈ 14971:2007's Annex C,
  Annex B ≈ its Annex G (techniques supporting risk analysis), Annex C is new
  (risk-management policy and acceptance criteria).
- One digest gives the Annex A question count as **46**, noting questions were
  *added* relative to the earlier edition (data storage, device autonomy). The
  older figure commonly quoted for 14971:2007 Annex C is 34. **Neither number is
  verified here**; treat "roughly 35–50 questions" as the honest range.
- Annex A carries, per one digest, an explicit instruction that **"the list of
  questions in Annex A should not be used as a check list"** — it is a
  systematic prompt, not a completeness test.

That last point is the strongest available evidence for the ticket's
classification-vs-identification distinction *from inside the standards family
itself*: the artefact people reach for when they say "ISO 14971 gives you a
hazard list" is a prompt list, in a Technical Report, that disclaims being a
checklist.

**Verdict:** open, enumerative, ~35–50 questions, **identification**, and
non-normative.

### 1.3 What does not transfer to a wellness app

Two of the four ISO 14971 category headings — **energy** and **biological and
chemical** — presuppose a physical device that stores energy or contacts tissue.
For a wellness app they are empty by construction. **Operational** and
**information** hazards transfer, and "information hazards" is the only one that
lands squarely on a health-content app. So of ISO 14971's four categories, one is
directly useful, one is partly useful, and two are dead weight. Anyone reusing
the 14971 headings in `RMP-001`'s wellness-app context is reusing a 1-of-4 hit
rate, and `RMP-001` `[LOCAL]` is explicit that it applies 14971 *principles*
"without claiming ISO 14971 medical-device compliance".

## 2. IEC 62366-1: the committee tried this and removed it

`[SRCH]` This is the most directly relevant finding in the note.

- **IEC 62366:2007 had a use-error taxonomy** — Annex D, built on the
  slip / lapse / mistake distinction (Reason's cognitive-stage error model), with
  a different mitigation strategy attached to each class (slips → button
  proximity/layout; lapses → prompts and checklists; mistakes → labels and
  affordances).
- **IEC 62366-1:2015 removed it.** Digests state the 2015 edition "no longer
  recognizes the taxonomy of errors from the previous standard", and give the
  reason as the class distinctions **not being reliably discriminable**: the
  worked example is a user entering mmol/l where mg/dl was expected — was that an
  error of attention (slip) or of knowledge (mistake)? The classification
  argument consumed the effort without changing the mitigation.
- **What replaced it is not a taxonomy but a construct**: the
  **hazard-related use scenario** — "a use scenario that could lead to a
  hazardous situation or harm" — plus **Annex B**, informative *examples* of
  hazards and hazardous situations related to usability (Table B.1). Examples,
  again, not a closed set.

So: IEC 62366-1:2015 supplies **no** use-error taxonomy, offers examples for
identification, and its predecessor's generative taxonomy was withdrawn on
grounds of inter-rater unreliability. If the wayfinder is weighing a generative
health-hazard set, this is the precedent that says what fails: not the
generation, the **filing**. A set generated by negating properties is easy to
justify and hard to apply consistently when two categories can both plausibly
hold one risk.

**Verdict (2015):** no taxonomy; Annex B examples, open, enumerative,
**identification**.
**Verdict (2007, withdrawn):** closed, generated from a cognitive-stage property
set, size 3, intended for **classification** — and abandoned for exactly the
reason classification taxonomies fail.

## 3. ISO/TS 82304-2:2021 — what it requires, and what `4.1-Q7` actually is

### 3.1 The standard

`[SRCH]` Corroborated across digests:

- The quality requirements are **Clause 5**, grouped into *Product information*
  plus **four quality aspects**: **Healthy and safe (5.2)**, **Easy to use
  (5.3)**, **Secure data (5.4)**, **Robust build (5.5)**. `[LOCAL]` agrees —
  the local checklist mirrors exactly this structure.
- There are **81 quality-requirement questions**, of which **67 (83%)** affect
  the four quality scores; the question set and the Annex B scoring method were
  derived by a **two-round Delphi study with 83 experts across six continents**,
  published open-access as Hoogendoorn P, Versluis A, van Kampen S, et al.,
  *"What Makes a Quality Health App—Developing a Global Research-Based Health App
  Quality Assessment Framework for CEN-ISO/TS 82304-2: Delphi Study"*, JMIR
  Formative Research 2023, doi:10.2196/43905. (Unread.)
- **Clause 4** is the assessment process. Digests conflict on 4.1's exact
  content: one says *"In Section 4.1 'Quality assessment', the health app
  manufacturer shall provide answers to the questions defined in Clause 5"*;
  another says 4.1 concerns how evidence is provided to assessment organisations
  and multi-platform apps.
- A hazard-identification requirement **does** exist in 82304-2, worded (per
  digest) *"the health app manufacturer shall identify and document known and
  foreseeable hazards to intended users in both normal and fault conditions
  through the introduction and use of the health app"*, annotated as **adapted
  from IEC 82304-1:2016, 4.1**, with a companion note on risk-estimation
  information sources **adapted from ISO 14971:2019, 5.5**. Note the ambiguity:
  *"adapted from IEC 82304-1:2016, 4.1"* is a provenance note about the **source**
  standard's clause 4.1 — it does not establish that the requirement sits in
  **82304-2's** clause 4.1. It may well sit in 5.2.2 (*Health risks & safety*).
  **Unresolved.**
- **No hazard categories, and no requirement to define any, appear in any
  digest of 82304-2.** What does appear is *examples* of health-app-specific
  risks in a note: **over-reliance, disproportionate attachment and addiction to
  the health app, manipulation affecting human autonomy**. That is the closest
  thing 82304-2 has to a health-hazard vocabulary, and it is three illustrative
  examples in a note, not a taxonomy.

**Verdict:** supplies no hazard-category taxonomy; requires identification and
documentation of hazards; offers three example risk *kinds* specific to apps.
Genuinely leaves categories open, as the ticket says.

### 3.2 `ISO-82304-2:4.1-Q7` is a local checklist item, not a clause

`[LOCAL]` This matters for the ticket's framing. `4.1-Q7` lives in
`/home/user/documentation/checklist/iso_checklist.txt`, an **org-authored**
checklist whose header reads:

```
# ISO/TS 82304-2 Health App Quality Checklist
# Source:
# - ISO/TS 82304-2:2021 Health software — Part 2: Health and wellness apps — Quality and reliability
```

Under `## 0.1 Quality Assessment (ISO 4.1)` it lists seven items `Q1`–`Q7`,
ending `ISO-82304-2:4.1-Q7 Hazard categories defined and documented`. The `Q`
numbering under clause 4 is almost certainly the checklist author's own
decomposition, not the standard's: the same file's clause-5 sections have gappy
numbering (`5.2.1-Q1, Q2, Q3, Q5`), which reads like a *selection* from the
standard's 81 numbered questions, whereas `4.1-Q1…Q7` is a dense run of
process-shaped statements ("Standardized assessment methodology documented",
"Qualified risk management team assigned") that look authored rather than quoted.

Consequences for the ticket:

- The sentence *"`ISO-82304-2:4.1-Q7` requires 'Hazard categories defined and
  documented'"* is true **of the checklist**. It is **not** established that
  ISO/TS 82304-2 requires hazard *categories* anywhere. The standard's
  requirement, as far as any digest shows, is to identify and document **hazards**.
- So "the obligation exists and the content is the organisation's to provide" is
  right in substance but the obligation is one the organisation wrote for itself.
  `rdm gap` reporting the clause covered is reporting coverage of a local
  checklist row.
- This does not weaken the case for defining categories — `RMP-001` `[LOCAL]`
  already classifies all risks under three of them and the risk clusters depend
  on it — but it means **no external authority constrains what the categories
  are, or that there be any**. There is no clause to conform to.

## 4. The Five Rights: a design heuristic, and no published safety taxonomy

`[SRCH]` Establishing provenance and use, separately.

**Provenance.** The Five Rights were first articulated by Osheroff in 2007;
digests give the origin publication as Osheroff JA, Teich JM, Middleton B,
Steen EB, Wright A, Detmer DE, *"A roadmap for national action on clinical
decision support"*, J Am Med Inform Assoc 2007;14:141–145, and the framework's
development in Osheroff's HIMSS books (*Improving Medication Use and Outcomes
with Clinical Decision Support*, 2009; *Improving Outcomes with Clinical Decision
Support: An Implementer's Guide*, 2nd ed., 2012). The canonical statement: CDS
delivers **the right information, to the right person, in the right format,
through the right channel, at the right time in the workflow**. All unread.

**How it is used.** Every source found uses it as a **design and implementation
heuristic**:

- AHRQ hosts it as *"Section 2 — Overview of CDS Five Rights"* inside its CDS
  implementation guidance (`digital.ahrq.gov`, blocked) — i.e. a chapter of an
  implementer's guide.
- Professional-body material frames it the same way: an AHIMA journal article
  (*The Five Rights of Clinical Decision Support: CDS Tools Helpful for Meeting
  Meaningful Use*), an ANIA session (*Optimizing Clinical Decision Support:
  Balancing the Five Rights without Compromising Clinical Value*), a CMS
  tipsheet.
- Applied-mapping papers exist (e.g. a table applying "AHRQ's Five Rights
  framework for CDS" to genomic CDS; *A Closer Look at the "Right" Format for
  Clinical Decision Support*, and *Right Idea, Wrong Time: Focusing on Alert
  Timing for Effective Decision Support*, PMID 40360000) — these evaluate a
  design against one Right. They are not taxonomies.

**Is it used as a safety taxonomy?** No source found does this. Searches
specifically targeting the negated form ("wrong information / wrong person /
wrong format / wrong channel / wrong time" as a classification), and targeting
"five rights" + hazard analysis / FMEA / failure modes, returned:

- **negation used rhetorically, in trade and vendor writing** — "an alert with
  the right information delivered to the wrong person, at the wrong time, or
  through the wrong format becomes noise rather than support", "when any of the
  five rights is missing, CDS effectiveness drops". Effectiveness framing, not
  harm framing, and no source that enumerates the negations as a set of
  categories to file risks under;
- **no peer-reviewed paper** mapping failure modes onto the five Rights as a
  taxonomy. The digest for that search says so explicitly.

**What the CDS-safety literature classifies instead is causes, not harms.** The
main published taxonomy is Wright A, et al., *"Clinical decision support alert
malfunctions: analysis and empirically derived taxonomy"*, J Am Med Inform Assoc
2018;25(5):496–506, doi:10.1093/jamia/ocx106 — 82 malfunction cases, 68
analysable, categories including **build error** (correct rule initialised with
wrong values — the most common), **conceptualization error**, **defect in EHR
software**, **environment migration**. That is an origin axis. It is precisely
the axis the ticket flags `RMP-001` for mixing into its Health/Safety examples
("incorrect health advice", "missed alerts" are origins; "user harm" is a harm).

**Verdict:** the Five Rights is closed (5), and it *is* generated from a property
set — the five properties a CDS intervention must have. But it is published and
used for **design**, and its use as a *safety* taxonomy has no citation. Anyone
adopting the negated form is adopting an original construction with a well-known
heuristic's vocabulary, not adopting prior art. (Per the ticket, no view is
offered here on whether that is a good idea.)

## 5. Everything else with a claim to STRIDE's structure

`[SRCH]` throughout this section.

### 5.1 The two genuine structural analogues (both identification, not classification)

**SHARD — Software Hazard Analysis and Resolution in Design.** A HAZOP
adaptation for computer-based systems, focused on **flows of information between
components** — sensor inputs, outputs to actuators and displays, and data moving
between functions. It applies a **small closed set of guide words** to each flow
and asks what deviation would be hazardous. The guide-word set reported is
**Omission, Commission, Early, Late, Value** — five, and generated: *does the
flow happen at all* (omission / commission), *when does it happen* (early /
late), *is the content right* (value). That is the same construction as STRIDE:
enumerate the properties a flow must have and negate each. Primary reference is
McDermid's *Software Hazard and Safety Analysis* (Springer, blocked); the SHARD
lineage is Pumfrey/McDermid at York. **Size 5, closed by construction, applied
per data flow, for identification.**

**STPA — unsafe control actions.** Leveson's Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis
generates, for each control action in a control structure, **four** ways it can
be unsafe: (1) a control action required for safety is **not provided**; (2) an
**unsafe** control action **is provided**; (3) a potentially safe action is
provided **too early, too late, or out of order**; (4) a safe action is
**stopped too soon or applied too long**. Generated from the properties of a
control action — existence, correctness, timing, duration. The **STPA Handbook**
(Leveson NG, Thomas JP, March 2018) is freely redistributable for non-commercial
use. STPA has an established healthcare literature (patient safety in the US
health system; eHealth interoperability conformance profiles; STPA-sec adapted
for health information cyber security; process-map-based control structures for
healthcare, Wong, *Risk Analysis* 2023). **Size 4, closed by construction,
applied per control action, for identification.**

Both are the shape the ticket is looking for — and **neither is a hazard-category
taxonomy.** They are per-element guide words: you run them once for every flow or
every control action and get a *list of candidate hazards*, not a set of bins to
file finished risks into. Adopting either as a hazard *category* set would be
using an identification instrument for a classification job, which is exactly
the confusion the ticket is trying to avoid.

**LINDDUN** is the proof the pattern generalises outside security: seven privacy
threat types — **Linking, Identifying, Non-repudiation, Detecting, Data
Disclosure, Unawareness, Non-compliance** — built at KU Leuven (DistriNet, first
published 2010) deliberately as STRIDE's privacy counterpart, generated by
negating privacy properties, applied over a data-flow diagram with threat-tree
catalogues. **Size 7, closed, generative, used for both identification (threat
trees per DFD element) and classification (the mnemonic as a filing set).** So
the STRIDE pattern has been successfully replicated once — for privacy. **No
equivalent replication for safety or health harms was found.** Security-safety
hybrids exist (SAHARA combines automotive HARA with STRIDE; FMVEA extends FMEA
with vulnerabilities) but they import STRIDE's *security* categories into a
safety process; they do not generate safety categories by negation.

### 5.2 Classification taxonomies (filing structures, mostly post-market)

| Artefact | Structure |
| --- | --- |
| **WHO ICPS** (International Classification for Patient Safety; *Towards an International Classification for Patient Safety: the conceptual framework*, PMID 19147595) | **10 high-level classes** — incident type, patient outcomes, patient characteristics, incident characteristics, contributing factors/hazards, organizational outcomes, detection, mitigating factors, ameliorating actions, actions taken to reduce risk. **"Incident type" itself has 13 categories.** Enumerated by expert drafting group, not generated. For **classification** of reported incidents. |
| **Magrabi et al.** (*Using FDA reports to inform a classification for health information technology safety problems*, JAMIA 2012;19(1):45; and the 2010 JAMIA precursor, 17(6):663) | **Two axes** × **32 preferred terms**. Axis 1: principal source — *machine-related* vs *human–machine interaction-related*. Axis 2: nature of the error — *input* (data entry), *output* (data retrieval), *transfer* (between systems), plus technical categories added later (software functionality, system configuration, device interface, network configuration). Example terms: *wrong input*, *(machine) not alerted*, *data loss*. **Partly generative** — the input/output/transfer axis is an information-flow property set, the same intuition as SHARD — but the 32 terms were derived empirically from incident reports ("natural categories" from a 25% sample), so the set is **open and evidence-grown, not closed by construction**. For **classification**. Widely reused (Wales, the Australian Commission's health-IT incident classification tool). |
| **Health IT Hazard Manager** (AHRQ / Abt Associates / Geisinger, Walker JM et al., beta-test final report 2012) | Four components per hazard — **discovery, causation, impact, corrective action** — with **8 causation categories** and a "Revised Hazard Manager Ontology" in the report's Appendix B. In the beta test, *Software Design → Faulty Design* was the single most common causal characteristic (189 of 495 hazards, 38%). Enumerated. For **classification**, by care providers, post-hoc. |
| **NCC MERP Taxonomy of Medication Errors** (free PDF, `nccmerp.org`) | Its *type of error* axis **is** a negation of the "rights" of medication administration: **wrong drug, wrong time, wrong strength/concentration, wrong dosage form/technique/route, improper dose/dose omission, wrong patient**. This is the one place found where negating a "rights" heuristic has become an actual published taxonomy — but for a **physical administration act**, in **care delivery**, for **classification** of reported errors, not for software design. |
| **Wright et al. CDS alert malfunction taxonomy** (see §4) | Cause axis, empirically derived, open. **Classification.** |
| **Sittig & Singh 8-dimension sociotechnical model** (*A new sociotechnical model for studying health information technology in complex adaptive healthcare systems*, PMID 20959322) | 8 dimensions — hardware/software infrastructure, clinical content, human–computer interface, people, workflow and communication, internal organizational features, external rules and regulations, measurement and monitoring. Explicitly **not independent, sequential or hierarchical**. A lens for analysis, enumerated, neither closed nor generated. |

### 5.3 The landscape number

A two-part systematic review — *Patient safety classifications, taxonomies and
ontologies* (J Biomed Inform; part 2 on content coverage, 2023, vol 148, art.
104549, PMID 37984548) — identified **77 classifications or ontologies across 89
papers**. 13 aim to cover all medical domains; domain-specific ones cluster on
medication (16), surgery (8), medical devices (3), general practice (3), primary
care (3). The most-covered concepts are *incident types* (41), *contributing
factors/hazards* (31), *patient outcomes* (29), *degree of harm* (25), *action*
(18).

Two things follow. First, **the field is saturated with classification
taxonomies** — 77 of them — and the review's own concept counts show they are
built around **incident type + contributing factor + outcome + degree of harm**,
i.e. post-market incident reporting, not pre-market design hazard filing.
Second, **none of the 77 is reported anywhere as having a generative closed
structure**; the review's framing is content coverage, which is the question you
ask about enumerated vocabularies.

### 5.4 What supplies nothing

- **NHS DCB0129 / DCB0160** (clinical risk management for health-IT
  manufacturers and deploying care organisations) require a **hazard log** with,
  per hazard, clinical impact, severity, likelihood, a risk score (1–5),
  controls, residual risk and review date. **No hazard categories are supplied.**
  The severity and likelihood scales are the standardised part; *what kinds of
  hazards there are* is left entirely to the Clinical Safety Officer. This is the
  closest regulatory analogue to Halla Health's position (software, no device
  claim, UK health context) and it confirms that "define your own categories" is
  the norm, not an omission.
- **Digital mental health / wellness-app harms literature** — directly relevant
  to a wellness app — has **no agreed taxonomy**. The systematic reviews (*The
  Safety of Digital Mental Health Interventions: Systematic Review and
  Recommendations*, PMC10594135; *Systematic review and meta-analysis of adverse
  events in clinical trials of mental health apps*, npj Digital Medicine 2024,
  doi:10.1038/s41746-024-01388-y; a narrative scoping review of ISRCTN-registered
  DMHI trials, PMC9996423) report harms ranging from mild (frustration with
  glitches, boredom) to severe (symptom deterioration, new symptoms, suicidal
  ideation), plus systemic harms such as widening digital-access disparities —
  and their central finding is **under-reporting and heterogeneity**: only 55 of
  171 identified trials reported adverse events at all, with inconsistent
  assessments and categories. The reviews call for guidelines that do not yet
  exist. So the domain closest to Halla Health has the *least* settled
  vocabulary, and there is no taxonomy here to adopt.

## 7. What adaptation would cost, by route

Reporting costs, not recommending routes.

- **Reuse ISO 14971's four headings.** Cheapest to cite, and the citation is to
  an *informative annex of a standard the organisation explicitly does not claim
  compliance with* `[LOCAL]`. Two of four categories are permanently empty for a
  wellness app, which makes the "closed set" claim hollow and invites the
  question of why the empty ones are there. Also: the headings are *sources of
  harm* (energy, chemicals, information), so filing "user acts on wrong advice"
  under them means filing a harm under an origin — the same axis-mixing defect
  the ticket already identifies in `RMP-001`.
- **Reuse an existing classification taxonomy** (WHO ICPS incident types,
  Magrabi's 32 terms, NCC MERP). All are open sets grown from incident reports
  in *care-delivery* settings, sized 13–32, and shaped for post-market reporting.
  Cost: an open vocabulary where the schema question is closed-vs-open, a size
  an order of magnitude beyond three categories, and a mapping exercise from
  "incident reported in a hospital" to "risk identified in a design review".
- **Adopt a guide-word set** (SHARD's 5, STPA's 4). Genuinely closed, genuinely
  generative, domain-neutral, freely citable (STPA Handbook; SHARD via the
  academic literature). Cost: they are the wrong *kind* of instrument — per-flow
  or per-control-action prompts, not hazard bins — so using them as categories
  means re-purposing them, and the citation would then be doing less work than
  it appears to.
- **Construct a set by negating a property set** (whatever the property set).
  No standard to cite: the construction is original, and the two prior attempts
  in this space point opposite ways — LINDDUN succeeded and is widely used;
  IEC 62366's slip/lapse/mistake taxonomy was **withdrawn by the committee for
  inter-rater unreliability**. The distinguishing factor between them, on this
  evidence, is that LINDDUN's categories are properties **of the system** while
  62366's were properties **of the user's cognition** — the former are
  observable from the artefact, the latter require inferring what a person was
  thinking. Any negation-derived health-hazard set should be tested against that:
  can two reviewers independently file the same risk in the same bin from the
  artefact alone?
- **Two levels vs one.** Nothing found requires or forbids either. The only
  two-level precedent with any structure is Magrabi's (source axis × nature
  axis), which is 2 × 16-ish and grew rather than being designed. WHO ICPS is
  the other (10 classes, one of which has 13 members) and is a reporting
  framework. Neither supports a claim that two levels is standard practice.

## 8. Things that cut against the ticket's framing

1. **The obligation the ticket rests on is self-authored.** `ISO-82304-2:4.1-Q7`
   *"Hazard categories defined and documented"* is a row in a local, org-written
   checklist `[LOCAL]`, not a clause of ISO/TS 82304-2. Nothing found shows the
   standard requiring hazard *categories* at all — only that hazards be
   identified and documented. `rdm gap` reporting the clause covered is reporting
   coverage of a checklist row against itself. That does not make categories
   unnecessary (the risk clusters need them), but it removes the external
   constraint the ticket implies, and it means the schema is free to choose.
2. **"The standards supply no list" is too strong for ISO 14971.** They supply
   four category headings — energy, biological and chemical, operational,
   information — plus dozens of examples. What they do not supply is a *closed,
   generated* set, or one that survives contact with a wellness app.
3. **A generative use-error taxonomy was tried in this exact standards family
   and withdrawn.** IEC 62366:2007's slip/lapse/mistake set was removed in the
   2015 edition because the class boundaries were not usable. This is the
   strongest empirical evidence available on what a negation-derived taxonomy
   costs, and it is a cost the ticket does not currently price.
4. **The property-negation pattern's one clear success in this neighbourhood is
   LINDDUN, for privacy — and it is bigger than three.** Seven categories,
   supported by threat-tree catalogues. If the argument for adapting STRIDE's
   *structure* leans on LINDDUN as precedent, it also inherits LINDDUN's
   apparatus: per-element threat trees, not just a mnemonic.
5. **The Five Rights' negation *has* a real published precedent — but it is
   NCC MERP's medication-error taxonomy, from the nursing "five rights of
   medication administration", not from Osheroff's CDS Five Rights.** So
   "negate the rights to get the error types" is an established move in patient
   safety; doing it to *Osheroff's* Rights is not. Worth knowing which precedent
   is actually available if the move is defended.
6. **The domain closest to Halla Health has the least settled vocabulary.** The
   digital-mental-health / wellness-app harm literature's headline finding is
   heterogeneous and absent adverse-event reporting, with explicit calls for
   guidelines that do not exist. Whatever is chosen will not be reconcilable with
   a field consensus, because there is not one.
7. **Identification instruments outnumber classification instruments in the
   standards, and the reverse holds in the research literature.** ISO 14971,
   ISO/TR 24971, IEC 62366-1, DCB0129 all give prompts and examples and no bins.
   The 77 classifications in the systematic review are almost all bins for
   post-market incidents. **There is essentially no prior art for a short closed
   set of hazard categories used to file pre-market design risks** — which is
   what `RMP-001`'s three categories are. That gap is the finding, and it is
   consistent with the ticket's suspicion that one must be adapted.

## 9. Open questions — what to re-check when the texts are reachable

1. **ISO/TS 82304-2:2021, clause 4.1 and clause 5.2.2 verbatim.** Does the
   standard require hazard *categories* anywhere, or only hazard identification?
   Does the word "category" appear in relation to hazards or residual risk? This
   decides whether §3.2 above is right that `4.1-Q7` is unanchored. Free routes:
   the ISO OBP preview (`iso:std:78182:en`), the iTeh sample PDF, and the
   ISO/TC215 committee draft `N3535` on the HL7 wiki.
2. **ISO 14971:2019 Annex C table numbering and the exact category names**, and
   whether Annex C states the lists are non-exhaustive. Every digest paraphrases;
   the numbering evidence actively conflicts.
3. **ISO/TR 24971:2020 Annex A question count** (34 vs 46) and the exact wording
   of the "should not be used as a check list" instruction — that sentence is the

   only paraphrased.

   dropping the 2007 use-error taxonomy. Everything in §2 about *why* it was

5. **The Hoogendoorn et al. Delphi paper (doi:10.2196/43905)** and the
